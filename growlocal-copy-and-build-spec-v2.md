## 0. Who we are, and how we now describe ourselves

**This replaces every previous description. The words "automation," "consultancy," "workflow," "AI" and "solution" do not appear anywhere on the flyer, the landing page or the product pages.**

We are a **growth agency for local business**. We help high-street businesses — garages, salons, clinics, agents, cafés — get more customers, keep more of them, and spend less of the week on things that shouldn't take a week. We do that by building practical tools around how each business actually runs, and by teaching owners what's working now so they can make better calls themselves.

The free tools are the honest, working sample of that. They're not bait. They're the smallest useful version of what we do.

**Boilerplate — use this wording wherever a short description is needed:**

> Grow Local is a growth agency for high-street business. We help local owners bring in more customers, run a tidier week, and keep more of what they earn.

**Tone rules.** Warm, plain, on their side. Write like someone who has stood behind a counter. Short sentences. Active voice. No exclamation marks. Never talk down. Never imply the reader is doing it wrong — always that there's a bit more available to them. UK spelling and pounds throughout.

## 1. The flyer — rebuilt from scratch

### 1.1 The thinking, briefly

The old flyer sold one tool. That was the flaw. It made Grow Local look like a single product, which meant a salon owner holding a garage flyer had no reason to scan, and a non-owner had no reason to pass it on.

The fix is to stop selling a tool and start showing **a collection**. A menu of free, practical things does three jobs at once: it proves there's something for any trade, it creates the specific curiosity we want ("which of these applies to me?"), and it makes the flyer *passable* — a customer glancing at it on a counter can see instantly that their brother-in-law with the plumbing business should have this.

The second decision is format. A flyer that must feel like a £25,000 piece of work and survive on premium stock should not be a single printed side. It should be a **card** — something with weight that gets kept rather than binned.

### 1.2 Format specification

**A6 card, double-sided, 400gsm uncoated stock**, deep-dyed or duplexed so the edge shows dark. Matte, no lamination — uncoated is what reads as expensive here, gloss undoes it instantly. If budget allows, a single spot of gold foil on the wordmark rule; if not, the Gold Leaf ink alone is fine.

3mm bleed, crop marks, 4-up on A4 for proofing but final run on the heavy stock.

**Hard rule: the front must work alone.** Assume half the people who see this never turn it over. Everything essential — what it is, that it's free, and the QR — lives on the front. The back is the reward for turning it over, and the reason it gets kept.

### 1.5 On universal versus trade-specific

**Recommendation: go universal, as you preferred.** Now that the flyer leads with a collection rather than a single tool, the reason for trade variants has largely gone — the menu does the targeting work by itself, and the reader self-selects. One design also means one print run, better stock for the same money, and no risk of handing a garage flyer to a dentist.

**One exception worth making.** Estate agents are different enough that several of the six tools don't apply, and the valuation tool is a much sharper hook than anything on the universal card. For a deliberate drop on a parade of agents, print a small run of a variant with identical design where:

- The headline becomes **"Five free tools for estate agents."**
- The sub-line becomes: *"Built for independent sales and lettings agents. The quiet work that turns a browser into an instruction."*
- Item `01` becomes Instant Valuation, followed by the property-specific tools listed in the companion document.

Everything else — bands, palette, contact block, "pass it on" line — stays exactly as the universal card.

### 1.6 Rejected concepts, and why

Recorded so the agent doesn't drift back toward them.

**"The customers you never knew you lost."** Strong hook, genuinely provocative — but it opens on loss and mild accusation, which is the wrong first note for a brand positioned as being on the owner's side. It also implies a single problem, and we're offering a set.

**"On the house."** Warm and disarming, and it was a good name candidate. As a flyer headline it's too oblique for a cold read — a card that doesn't say what it is within two seconds gets binned.

**A pure teaser: wordmark, one line, QR, nothing else.** Looks the most expensive of any option and would be the right call for a brand people already know. For a cold drop it's a gamble — specificity is what earns the scan here, and the menu on the back is exactly what makes the card worth keeping.

Three beats, each one a thing an owner actually wants, and the third handles the objection before it forms. It's benefit-led rather than category-led, and it's instantly obvious to someone arriving cold from a card they picked up off a counter.

### 2.5a SECTION 4b — Who's behind this (Bone field, narrow measure)

**The decision: no separate About page in the main navigation, but yes to a short About band on the landing page, plus a full `/about` page linked only from the footer.**

The reasoning. A visitor arriving from a flyer has one question — *is this legitimate, or is someone harvesting my email?* — and they'll answer it in about four seconds from tone and specificity, not from a page they'd have to click to. Sending them off to an About page to resolve that doubt loses most of them; they don't come back. But a short, specific, human band inline resolves it without costing a click.

The full `/about` page still earns its place, because the people who look for it are the highest-intent visitors on the site — the ones considering paid work. Footer link only. It should never compete with the tools for attention.

**Placement:** directly after "How it works," before the enquiry form. Narrow measure — roughly 60 characters — so it reads as a personal note rather than a marketing block.

### 2.7a Brand — the rule

**One brand, everywhere, with no second name anywhere on any customer-facing surface.**

### 2.8 Design notes on the existing page

The current build is close. Keep the fascia band, the Gold Leaf rules, the serif/sans pairing, the editorial tool rows and the phone mockup — all of it is working. Four refinements:

1. **The tool list is six items and stays that way for now.** No filter, no categories, no search. At this length a plain, well-spaced editorial list outperforms any navigation aid, because every added control is one more decision before the click that matters. Revisit only if the library passes about ten tools.
2. **Give the hero more vertical room.** It's currently a little tight against the fold. The headline is the page's strongest asset and should breathe.
3. **Add a slim sticky header** on scroll — wordmark left, `See the tools` text-link right, Bottle green, 56px. Six tools means more scrolling, and the way back up needs to be permanent.
4. **Section 2 should feel handwritten in spirit.** Consider setting it at a slightly larger body size than the rest of the page, with a wider left margin, so it reads as an aside rather than a section.

## 4. Product page — Instant Quote Form

**Hero** · `GARAGES · TRADES · ANY BUSINESS THAT QUOTES`
> **Give a price before they ring the next number.**
> Most people checking you out online won't call to ask what something costs. They'll guess, or they'll ask someone else. This gives them a ballpark in seconds — and gives you their name and number.
CTA: **Build my quote form** · `FREE · NO SIGN-UP · READY IN 60 SECONDS`

**The problem**
> **Your quietest hours are costing you the most.**
> Someone finds you at eleven at night. They want to know roughly what a job costs. There's no price anywhere, the phone's shut, and ringing a stranger to ask feels like a commitment. So they close the tab and try the next name on the list.
> You never hear about those people. No missed call, no voicemail, nothing to follow up. It's the most invisible way a local business loses work, and it happens every week.

**What you get**
> **A quote form with your prices on it** — Your jobs, your ranges, your business name. Not a generic calculator.
> **A QR code you can print** — For the counter, the window, the van, the invoice.
> **A link you can send** — Text it mid-call, put it on your website, add it to your Google profile.
>
> Every enquiry arrives with a name, a number and the job they wanted. Including the ones that come in at midnight.

**The generator** · `BUILD YOURS`
> **Takes about a minute.**
> Add your business name, check the prices we've suggested, change anything that isn't right. Nothing's set in stone — come back and rebuild it whenever.

**Where to use it** — *Five places this earns its keep.*
> Most people put it in one spot and forget it. These are the placements that actually bring work in, roughly in order of what they're worth.

**1. Your Google Business Profile** — *it's where most people find you, and most profiles have nothing to click.*
1. Sign in to your Google Business Profile and open **Edit profile**.
2. Paste your link into **Appointment link** (or Website, if that's empty).
3. Add a post saying "Get an instant price" with the same link. Posts run for seven days, so repeat monthly.

**2. Your website** — *a visitor who wanted a price and found none is a visitor you've lost.*
1. Add a homepage button reading **Get an instant price**.
2. Put the link in your footer so it's on every page.

**3. Mid-call, by text** — *someone ringing for a price is the warmest lead of your day.*
1. Save the link as a text shortcut on your phone.
2. Send it while you're still talking, for anything complicated.
3. They get something in writing, and you get their number automatically.

**4. The counter and the window** — *catches people who walk past when you're shut.*
1. Print at A5 for the window, at eye height.
2. Print smaller for the counter.
3. One line above it: "Scan for an instant price."

**5. Invoices and vehicles** — *past customers are the cheapest work you'll ever win.*
1. Add the QR to your invoice footer.
2. Put it on the van. People photograph vans at traffic lights.

**Common mistakes** — *Three things worth avoiding.*
> **Hiding prices completely.** The worry is a competitor seeing them, or being held to a number. A range answers both. "£149–£219" is enough to start a conversation without committing you to anything.
> **Ranges set too narrow.** Precise-looking, but it backs you into a corner. Roughly 40% between low and high is honest and still useful.
> **One placement, then forgotten.** The businesses getting real numbers from this have it in four or five places. Twenty minutes, once.

**What to expect**
> This won't transform your business overnight, and anyone saying otherwise is selling something.
> What it does is stop a specific leak. Most businesses that put it in a few places pick up a handful of enquiries a month they'd never have known about — usually the after-hours ones. Whether that's worth it depends on what an average job is worth to you.
> It costs nothing and takes a minute, so the maths is usually straightforward.

**Need a hand?** — opening sentence:
> If you'd rather not fiddle about with your Google profile or your website, we'll talk you through it.

**Related:** Google Review QR · Foundation Scorecard · Booking Link

## 5. Product page — Google Review QR

**Hero** · `ANY BUSINESS WITH CUSTOMERS`
> **Ask while they're still smiling.**
> The best moment to ask for a review is about ninety seconds after someone's happy — not three days later by email, when they've forgotten. This makes that moment take one tap.
CTA: **Build my review QR**

**The problem**
> **You're getting a fraction of the reviews you've earned.**
> Think about last week. How many people left genuinely pleased? Now check how many reviews you got. That gap isn't a service problem — it's a timing problem.
> People will happily leave a review. They just won't remember later, and they won't go hunting for where. Ask them while they're stood in front of you, with the link already open, and most will.

**What you get**
> **A QR that opens your review page** — Straight to the review box. No searching, no menus.
> **A link you can text** — For anyone you finish with off-site.
> **A printable version** — Counter, table, receipt, mirror.
>
> Nothing to install, nothing for your customer to download.

**The generator**
> **Takes about a minute.**
> Enter your business name, check we've matched the right Google listing, and it's ready to print or send.

**Where to use it** — *Six places, and when to ask.*
> Placement matters less than timing. The rule underneath all of it: ask when they're happiest, which is almost always at the end, in person.

**1. The counter or reception desk** — *where the transaction ends is where they're most pleased.*
1. Print at A6 and stand it where they pay.
2. Add one line: "Enjoyed your visit? Two taps, thank you."
3. Have staff mention it as they hand over the card machine. This alone tends to double results.

**2. The receipt or invoice** — *the one piece of paper they definitely take home.*
1. Add the QR to your receipt footer.
2. Keep it 20mm square or larger so it scans reliably.

**3. By text, straight after** — *for anyone you don't finish with face to face.*
1. Save the link as a shortcut on the business phone.
2. Send within the hour, not the next day.
3. Keep it short: "Thanks again — if you've a spare minute, a review really helps us."

**4. The table or the treatment room** — *a relaxed, captive moment.*
1. Restaurants: a small card per table, or on the bill folder.
2. Salons and clinics: on the mirror, or the wall opposite the chair.

**5. Your window** — *signals confidence, catches regulars walking past.*
1. Print at A5, eye height.
2. Pair it with your star rating if it's good.

**6. Your email signature** — *free, permanent, two minutes.*
1. One line: "Happy with our work? Leave us a review," with the link behind it.

**Pull-quote** (Gold Leaf rules above and below)
> You're allowed to ask. Most owners feel awkward about it and never do, which is exactly why their review count doesn't match their quality. "If you've got a minute, it really does help us" is enough. The one thing you mustn't do is offer anything in return — discounts or freebies for reviews break Google's rules and can get the reviews stripped out.

**Teaching block — "Ask the happy ones, and ask them today"** (Bone panel, Gold Leaf keyline, sits directly beneath the pull-quote)

*This block replaces a tool we deliberately chose not to build. It's worth reading the note at §5a below before amending any of this wording.*

> **Why timing decides your rating**
>
> Here's something most owners never work out. The customers who leave reviews unprompted are overwhelmingly the annoyed ones — frustration is a much stronger motivator than satisfaction. Meanwhile the fifty people who were perfectly happy say nothing, because nothing happened worth telling anyone about.
>
> So a listing left to its own devices doesn't show what your business is like. It shows what your worst weeks were like.
>
> The fix isn't filtering anybody or hiding anything. It's simply asking everyone, while the visit is still fresh. Do that consistently and your rating drifts towards the truth, because the quiet majority finally gets counted.
>
> **What that means in practice:**
> · Ask on the day, not the week after. After about 24 hours, response rates fall off a cliff.
> · Ask everyone, every time — not just the ones you're confident about.
> · A steady trickle beats a burst. Ten reviews over ten weeks is worth more to Google, and looks more credible to a reader, than ten in an afternoon.
> · One poor review inside forty recent good ones barely registers. The same review inside six does real damage. Volume is your protection.
>
> And if someone's unhappy, ask them anyway. A fair, calm reply underneath a critical review persuades more readers than a spotless listing does — because a listing with nothing but five stars is the one people quietly assume is fake.

### 5a — A note for the agent on what we chose not to build

There is a common feature, often sold as a "feedback filter" or "review gate," which asks a customer how their visit went and routes the happy ones to Google while diverting the unhappy ones to a private form.

**We are not building it, and no page may imply we have.** It breaches Google's review policies, and listings using it can have reviews removed retroactively — which would leave a business we were trying to help worse off than before we arrived.

The teaching block above deliberately takes the legitimate half of that idea — *ask satisfied customers promptly and consistently* — and discards the illegitimate half. Keep that distinction intact in any future copy. If wording ever drifts toward screening, selecting or filtering customers before asking, it has gone wrong and needs pulling back.

**Common mistakes** — *Four things worth fixing.*
> **Asking days later, by email.** The feeling's faded and the email is one of forty. The same request, in person, at the end of a visit, performs many times better.
> **Sending people to your website instead of the review box.** Every extra click loses people. The link should open the stars, ready to tap.
> **Having the QR but never mentioning it.** A code nobody points at gets almost no scans. The words do the work; the QR just removes the friction.
> **Offering a discount for a review.** Against Google's rules, and those reviews can be removed. Asking plainly works better anyway.

**What to expect**
> Reviews build slowly. What changes quickly is the rate — businesses that ask properly, at the right moment, tend to go from a handful a year to a handful a month.
> That matters because Google's local results weigh how many reviews you have and how recent they are. And because a business with sixty recent reviews gets chosen more often than one with nine from 2021. It's rarely the reason someone picks you. It's very often the reason they don't.

**Need a hand?** — opening sentence:
> If your Google listing isn't verified, or you're not sure it's showing the right details, that's usually a fifteen-minute fix and we're glad to point you at it.

## 12. Build rules

1. Follow `growlocal-design-direction.md` exactly — palette, type, and the no-gradient / no-stock-photo / no-drop-shadow rules.
2. Mobile first. Most arrivals are a phone scan in a busy workplace. Test at 360px before anything else.
3. The generator is reachable without scrolling past education, on every product page.
4. No cookie banner unless analytics genuinely require one. Prefer privacy-friendly analytics.
5. Under 500KB per page. Fonts preloaded and subset. No video.
6. WCAG AA contrast, visible Gold Leaf focus states, correct heading order, labelled fields, `prefers-reduced-motion` honoured.
7. One CTA style per page. Deep Brass is the primary action only.
8. **Contact is text-first everywhere.** Never lead with "call us." The order is always: text, then email, then voicemail as the fallback.
9. Copy is final — use it verbatim. If a line genuinely won't fit, flag it rather than rewriting. The tone is deliberate and easily lost.
10. Before building, produce a short rationale covering flyer front/back structure, the six-item tool list, and mobile layout, and confirm it against this document.
